\begin{abstract}
\noindent
\texttt{edikit} is a Python library for building and auditing national
social accounting matrices (SAMs) from official statistics. It brings
established methods (supply-use reconciliation, concordances, and RAS
and GRAS balancing) into one documented workflow that reports every change
it makes to the data. At each stage the library computes and reports the
accounting identities, and it classifies accounts only through explicit
mappings. It returns every balancing adjustment cell by cell, reports and
marks the entry that closes an account, and can verify registered inputs
against SHA-256 manifests. The evidence comes from the package's scripted
workflows, or ``recipes.'' One builds balanced macro SAMs from
Eurostat's harmonized data. It is shown for Belgium, Denmark, and France;
for Spain, where it flags government accounts missing from current data;
and in a coverage test across all 37 candidate countries. Another audits a distributed SAM against published
controls, shown for the 2019 SAM for Ghana. A third reproduces a
separately reported cell-level reconstruction of the 2019 South African
SAM. The examples show that the software runs as documented, that its
balanced outputs are accounting-consistent, and that it reports when
balancing fails, as for Spain. They do not establish a general procedure for
constructing a national SAM. Outside the Eurostat datasets, the library
supplies the components for building one from national supply and use
tables (readers, concordances, explicit classification, and balancing),
while the construction rules remain country-specific and require source
interpretation and economic judgment. The package has one required
dependency, \texttt{openpyxl}, and is installable from PyPI.
\end{abstract}

\section{Overview}

\subsection{Introduction}

A social accounting matrix (SAM) is a square table recording, for one
economy and one year, every payment made from one account to another~\cite{pyatt1977}. Rows and columns carry the same ordered list of
accounts: activities that produce, commodities that are produced, factors
of production such as labor and capital, and institutions such as
households, corporations, and government, together with capital
accumulation and the rest of the world. The cell in row $i$, column $j$
holds the payment from account $j$ to account $i$, so a row gives an
account's receipts and a column its outlays. For every account, the two
totals must agree, as every payment is someone else's income.
National SAMs range from a few dozen accounts to several hundred and
follow the conventions of the System of National Accounts~\cite{sna2008}.

The matrix is a database, not a model, but much applied economy-wide
policy analysis rests on it~\cite{breisinger2009}. Multiplier analysis
traces how a change in demand propagates through the structure it
records, and computable general equilibrium models are calibrated so that
they reproduce the SAM in their base year before any policy is simulated.
Ministries of finance, central banks, development banks, and research
institutes use such models to assess tax reforms, tariff changes, and
public-investment programs. A model inherits whatever the matrix contains,
so an error in a cell carries through, usually unnoticed, into results
read as policy advice.

Building a SAM is a data-reconciliation exercise~\cite{round2003}. The
inputs are official statistics: supply and use tables
(SUTs),\footnote{Supply and use tables are the pair of matrices national
statistical offices publish to describe production and its disposition.
The supply table records which industries produce which products; the use
table records which industries and which final users consume them,
together with the value added generated in production~\cite{eurostat2008sut}. Together they satisfy the identities that anchor a
SAM's production block: the total supply of each product equals its total
use, and each industry's output equals its intermediate consumption plus
value added.} national and sector accounts, balance-of-payments
statistics, and household and labor force surveys. Each source has its own
classification, units, and vintage, and covers only part of what the
matrix needs. The analyst who builds the matrix assigns each source
category to a SAM account, estimates the cells that no source reports, and
chooses between sources that give different figures for the same flow. The
assembled matrix then does not balance, because independent sources do
not satisfy the identity by construction, and a final step adjusts cells
until receipts equal payments everywhere. This is usually done by the
biproportional method known as RAS (the name is not an abbreviation) or by
cross-entropy estimation~\cite{robinson2001}. Even an unusually well-documented SAM, such as the 2019 South African
matrix of van Seventer and Davies~\cite{vanseventer2023}, comes as a
matrix with a technical note and no code to regenerate it from its
sources.

\texttt{edikit} was written for that problem, during a cell-level
replication audit of the van Seventer and Davies matrix, published by the
United Nations University World Institute for Development Economics
Research (UNU-WIDER) and reported separately~\cite{abalo2026}. This paper
describes the software itself. It sets out which properties of a matrix
the software guarantees and which it leaves to the analyst, and it
presents evidence that the software works as documented. None of the
worked examples reported here appears in that earlier study.

\texttt{edikit} adds no new SAM mathematics. RAS, generalized RAS (GRAS)
for matrices with negative entries~\cite{junius2003,temurshoev2013},
cross-entropy balancing, and the accounting identities are long
established, and tools for them already exist. They include published
General Algebraic Modeling System (GAMS) programs for cross-entropy SAM estimation~\cite{robinson2000gams},
extensions of RAS for conflicting information~\cite{lenzen2009} and for
joint supply-use balancing~\cite{temurshoev2011}, collaborative
laboratories that compile and balance input--output systems at scale~\cite{lenzen2014}, the compilation methodology behind Eurostat's
inter-country supply-use tables~\cite{eurostat2019figaro}, and
general-purpose fitting packages such as \texttt{mipfp}~\cite{barthelemy2018}. Two are especially close to this work. The European
Commission's Joint Research Centre builds SAMs for EU member states from
Eurostat's supply-use tables and sector accounts~\cite{fuentes2017}, and
the R package \texttt{iotables} retrieves and processes Eurostat's
symmetric input--output tables~\cite{antal2025iotables}. Further
downstream, \texttt{pymrio} analyzes compiled multi-regional input--output
databases~\cite{stadler2021}, and \texttt{MARIO} builds and analyzes
input--output models from supply-use and input--output tables~\cite{tahavori2023}. The scope of \texttt{edikit} is narrower. It keeps a matrix's whole path,
from registered source files to published output, inside one open Python
library: it reads the sources, reports which accounting identities fail,
maps accounts only by explicit rule, logs every balancing adjustment, and
verifies that the inputs are the registered ones. It is not a general SAM-construction system, and it does not implement
cross-entropy estimation.

\subsection{Implementation and architecture}

\texttt{edikit} is pure Python (about 1{,}630 statements) with one required
external dependency, \texttt{openpyxl}, for Excel input and output. It has
four packages. Figure~\ref{fig:arch} shows the packages, the inputs each
reader accepts, the outputs the pipelines write, the entry points that call
them, and how each check handles failure.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{edikit.data}: functions that read official input files.
  Each reader handles one publisher's file format. They
  cover SUT workbooks in
  the layout of Statistics South Africa; supply-use tables
  (\emph{tableaux des ressources et des emplois}, TRE) in the layout of
  Senegal's Agence Nationale de la Statistique et de la D\'emographie
  (ANSD); SAM workbooks in Excel; Eurostat JSON-stat responses; and the
  bulk series files of the South African Reserve Bank (SARB), with an evaluator
  for signed series formulas. The package also holds concordances and
  \texttt{provenance}, which verifies files against a SHA-256 manifest. A
  source in another format needs a new reader.
  \item \texttt{edikit.model}: the list of account types (activity,
  commodity, factor, household, and so on) and RAS and GRAS balancing.
  Each account's type comes from a table of rules the user writes, never
  from guessing at its label. \texttt{classify\_by\_prefix} applies the
  table and stops with an error on any account that no rule covers. The
  balancing routines return the balanced matrix together with the factor
  by which each cell was scaled.
  \item \texttt{edikit.pipeline}: \texttt{eurostat\_sam} generates,
  balances, and reports a macro SAM for country-years covered by the
  European System of Accounts (ESA~2010) transmission datasets~\cite{eurostat2013esa}. \texttt{audit} reads a distributed SAM in CSV,
  checks its structure, and compares its macro aggregates with published
  national-accounts controls. \texttt{reconcile} places two producers'
  SAMs of the same economy on a common macro grid.
  \item \texttt{edikit.report}: Excel workbooks whose totals and balance
  checks are live formulas instead of pasted values, so a reader can trace
  them in a spreadsheet program.
\end{itemize}

Table~\ref{tab:checks} sorts the checks into two groups: those that stop
the run and those that record a finding and let it continue. The split is
deliberate. A published table can fail an accounting identity simply
because the publisher has withheld confidential cells. Stopping on such a
failure would make the table unusable, so the failure is recorded instead,
which documents it. Problems that would silently corrupt a result, such as
an identifier that appears twice or industries counted twice, stop the
run. The double-counting check allows a
5~percent margin because a source's own detail can exceed its published
total, at the cost of missing a double-counted section smaller than that.
Parsing and checking are kept apart: the readers only parse, the
\texttt{check\_*} functions then test the parsed table against its
identities, and a pipeline writes its report when its
\texttt{write\_report} is called, as the recipes do. Every check either
raises an exception or returns a finding, and none relies on
\texttt{assert}, so running Python with \texttt{-O} removes none of them.

Provenance is recorded partly by hand. A \texttt{SOURCES.md} file states
what each source is, who publishes it, how it was obtained, and under what
license. Whether a local file is the registered one is checked
automatically by \texttt{verify\_sources}, which compares each file with a
manifest in \texttt{sha256sum} format and raises on a missing or altered
file. \texttt{write\_manifest} generates such a manifest, and standard
tools (\texttt{shasum -a 256 -c}) read the same file. A script must call
this verification explicitly, since neither \texttt{fetch} nor the recipes
do.

The Eurostat pipeline embeds four economic choices.

\emph{Instrument accounts.} Distributive transactions (property income,
current taxes on income and wealth, social contributions and benefits,
other current transfers, pension-entitlement adjustments, and capital
transfers) are routed through instrument accounts: each institutional
sector pays into an instrument account and receives from it. The sector
accounts record what each sector pays and receives but not to whom, so the
routing preserves every sector's totals without inventing bilateral flows.
A model that uses the matrix will nonetheless distribute a pooled account's
receipts in fixed shares, which amounts to assuming proportional
from-whom-to-whom flows.

\emph{Account residuals and their structure.} Before balancing, the
institutional accounts carry residuals. The report sets beside each
residual the items computed from the inputs that bear on it, together with
what they leave unexplained. Some items are cross-source differences: the
supply-use tables and the sector accounts each measure gross value added,
compensation of employees, operating surplus, and household consumption,
and the report shows both measurements with each dataset's vintage. Others
follow from the account design. Net capital transfers stay in the
institutions' accounts, because the savings--investment account receives
saving before capital transfers. For EU members, part of the taxes on
production and imports, mainly customs duties, is paid to EU institutions,
which the sector accounts treat as part of the rest of the world. The
generator credits all such taxes, net of the subsidies the rest of the
world pays, to the domestic government, so the government account carries
the difference. Purchases less sales of non-produced assets, such as land
and licenses, are not represented, so the savings--investment account
carries their net value. The examples show how far these items go. In
all four generation examples (Table~\ref{tab:examples}), the
residual of the labor account equals the difference between the two
datasets' figures for compensation of employees, and in three of the four,
the residual of the corporations' account equals their net capital
transfers. When Eurostat publishes a sector-account item neither as paid
nor as received, the generator sets it to zero and lists it, with a
warning that any account it feeds is unreliable.

\emph{The closing entry.} The capital factor account is closed by a
residual. Gross operating surplus and mixed income enter it from the
supply-use tables, the sector accounts distribute their own total to
domestic sectors, and the difference is booked against the rest of the
world. This closing entry measures the gap between the two datasets'
operating-surplus figures and records no observed flow. Because it is booked
against the rest of the world, it also appears in that account's
residual. Every report states it, and the balanced CSV marks it.

\emph{Balancing.} Each account needs one target. The default is the mean
of its receipts and payments, which treats the two sides as equally
reliable; receipts alone or payments alone can be chosen instead with the
\texttt{-{}-targets} option. Before RAS, a negative cell is replaced by a positive
flow in the opposite direction, which keeps each account's net position but
changes its gross totals. A negative closing entry therefore reappears
after balancing as a positive flow from the rest of the world to the
capital factor account, where it reads like factor income from abroad; the
balanced CSV's \texttt{note} column marks such cells. The library's GRAS
keeps negative entries in place; the pipeline uses the flip and RAS
instead. Balancing also changes aggregates the sources publish consistently:
gross domestic product (GDP) moves even where the supply-use block has no
identity findings, and no cell or block can be held fixed.

Three documented \emph{recipes} serve as entry points. \texttt{build/}
generates and balances a macro SAM for a country-year
(\texttt{python build\_sam.py -{}-country DK -{}-year 2019}).
\texttt{audit/} audits a distributed SAM in CSV
(\texttt{python audit\_sam.py -{}-sam matrix.csv -{}-kinds kinds.csv
-{}-controls controls.csv}). \texttt{replicate/} documents how the
scripts of the South African reconstruction reproduce its reported
numbers. The same steps take a few lines of Python:

{\small
\begin{verbatim}
from edikit.pipeline import eurostat_sam as es

paths = es.fetch("BE", 2019, "data/")     # three requests, cached
res   = es.generate("BE", 2019, paths)    # identities computed and recorded
bal, flipped = es.balance(res)            # RAS; every factor in bal.factors
es.write_report(res, "BE_2019_generation.md",
                balance=bal, flipped=flipped)
es.write_balanced_csv(bal, "BE_2019_macro_sam_balanced.csv",
                      flipped=flipped, plugs=res.plugs)
\end{verbatim}}

\subsection{Quality control}

\emph{Automated tests.} The public test suite has 78 tests. Many of them
use accounting identities as the check: they test whether the readers,
the account classification, RAS and GRAS, and the audit produce results
in which, for example, supply equals use and each account's receipts
equal its payments. Other tests run the whole generation and balancing
pipeline on a small invented economy with two industries, built so that
every identity holds exactly, so any departure signals an error. Each check in Table~\ref{tab:checks} is exercised by at least one
test, including every type of finding the identity checks record, as are
the residual diagnostics, the CSV notes, the rounding test, the
alternative balancing targets, and the output precision. Line coverage is
95~percent (90--99~percent per module). Coverage shows only which code
paths run, so the evidence of accounting correctness comes from the
identity-based and end-to-end tests. Continuous
integration runs the suite on Linux, macOS, and Windows with Python
3.11--3.13 for every pull request and every push to the main branch. The
wheel installed by \texttt{pip install edikit} contains the library only;
the tests are included in the source distribution and the repository.

\emph{Worked examples.} Every input the worked examples need is
distributed in the repository and registered in SHA-256 manifests, so
each example runs offline and reproduces the outputs stored with it. Table~\ref{tab:examples} summarizes them
together with the coverage test, which fetches current data. They cover
two tasks: generation within a harmonized statistical system, and the
audit of a matrix someone else built. They test execution and accounting
consistency and leave open whether the construction rules produce the
economically preferred matrix.

The generation inputs are Eurostat's supply table, use table, and
non-financial sector accounts~\cite{eurostat_naio15,eurostat_naio16,eurostat_nasa}. Eurostat revises
them, so the same command run later can give different results, and every
report records the vintage of each dataset. In the October data, seven
countries (Austria, Germany, Hungary, Italy, Portugal,
Spain, and Sweden) lack nine general-government sector-account items, and
their reports name them. A country passes when it has zero identity
findings, full commodity closure, no supply-use cross-differences, an
import identity within tolerance, no suppressed detail, no unpublished
sector-account items, converged balancing, no residual above 5~percent of
GDP, and no factor more than 100~percent away from one. Passing does not
mean balancing changed little: France passes although one of its cells
was scaled by 0.68, and several countries that need review have residuals
above 10~percent of GDP.

The balancing target matters at the aggregate level. For Belgium, GDP
after balancing moves by $+0.86$, $+0.90$, and $+0.94$~percent with
receipts, mean, and payments targets, respectively. The matrix CSVs round
values to EUR~0.1~million and factors to six decimals, so a balanced matrix
closes to the solver's tolerance in memory but only to that rounding in
the file; \texttt{-{}-decimals} writes a higher-precision export. The
toolkit's text reports are written with LF line endings on every
platform, so the reports, like the CSVs, are byte-identical across
operating systems.

For Ghana, the audit uses the SAM published by the Ghana Statistical
Service, the Institute of Statistical, Social and Economic Research, and
the International Food Policy Research Institute (IFPRI)~\cite{gss2023ghana,gss2023ghanapaper}, in GHS million, with controls
from the World Bank's World Development Indicators~\cite{worldbank2026wdi}. The audit locates departures from the controls
but cannot explain them. The aggregates also depend on the
account classification and on the audit's definitions. Private
consumption, for example, includes households' purchases from activities
as well as from commodities. For matrices distributed in rounded values,
the audit tests each account's imbalance against the most rounding could
produce: half the rounding unit (the grain) for each nonzero off-diagonal
cell in the account's row and column. The bound omits cells rounded to
zero, so it can understate the rounding error.

\emph{The South African reconstruction.} The library was first used for a
cell-level reconstruction of the 2019 South African SAM from public
sources, informed by the benchmark it audits~\cite{abalo2026}. That
reconstruction reproduces the benchmark exactly (to its rounding unit of
ZAR~0.5~million) in the production structure of all 61
activities, in the 316 final-demand cells, and in 43 of 45 computable
macro cells, and agrees within ZAR~0.5~million on 62.5~percent of the 6{,}732
cells common to the two matrices. Where public sources do not reach the
benchmark's household, occupation, and capital detail, it uses
survey-based shares. Because the reconstruction is benchmark-informed, it
bears on the benchmark's reproducibility and cannot independently
validate \texttt{edikit}, so it is not counted here. Reproducing it
requires third-party inputs that cannot be redistributed; the repository
records how to obtain each one and the digest needed to verify each
downloaded file.

\section{Reuse potential}

The package can be reused at three levels, set out below: parts that work
for any economy, parts that work only within a particular statistical
system such as Eurostat's, and work that cannot be automated because it
requires the analyst's own economic judgment.

\emph{Parts that work for any economy.} These need no change: the account types and rule-based classification, RAS and
GRAS balancing with the adjustment factor of every cell, concordances that
refuse duplicate mappings and report gaps, source verification, the Excel
workbooks with live formulas, and the audit of any SAM saved as a CSV
file. A modeler who uses a SAM built by someone else can audit it with two
small files, one classifying its accounts and one giving the published
national-accounts totals. The audit shows whether the matrix balances,
whether its imbalances could come from rounding, and how its totals
compare with the national accounts.

\emph{Parts tied to a statistical system.} The Eurostat generator works
only for country-years the ESA~2010 transmission datasets cover, and its
results depend on the data vintage. The committed coverage table records
which countries pass and which data it used. Each reader is tied to one
publisher's layout.

\emph{Work that requires the analyst's judgment.} Outside a harmonized system,
building a SAM means writing readers for the national sources, mapping
their classifications onto SAM accounts, choosing how to allocate flows
the sources do not report, choosing balancing targets, and deciding how to
treat conflicts between sources. The South African replication scripts
work through these decisions for one country. They show the shape of the
work but cannot simply be reconfigured for another country.
\texttt{edikit} records such decisions and makes them checkable, and the
analyst still has to make them.

Table~\ref{tab:recipes} summarizes the recipes. Someone reading a report
from the build recipe should work through it in four steps. First, check
whether any sector-account figures are missing from Eurostat's data,
because the generator sets them to zero and any account that uses them is
unreliable. Second, read the table comparing the supply-use tables with
the sector accounts, and the items listed beside each account's residual:
together they show where the two datasets disagree and which residuals
come from the way the accounts are set up. Third, look at the failed
accounting identities; in a table with confidential cells withheld, these
usually reflect the withholding rather than an error. Last, look at the
balancing factors: a factor far from one marks a cell that balancing
changed substantially, which should be examined before the matrix is
used.

A balanced macro SAM is not a
disaggregated SAM, and it may need further disaggregation by industry,
household group, or factor before it suits a particular modeling
application. Where several admissible mappings exist, an
accounting-consistent output need not be the economically preferred one;
the toolkit shows the chosen mapping and every adjustment but does not
certify them. Statistical confidentiality can prevent full
reconstruction, because suppressed cells can be detected and quantified
but not recovered. Balancing
cannot hold reliable cells or blocks fixed or weight cells by reliability,
as the KRAS extension of RAS does~\cite{lenzen2009}. Finally, \texttt{edikit} does not
implement cross-entropy estimation against a prior~\cite{robinson2001},
which matters most where margins are only partly known or further
constraints apply.

Natural extensions include GRAS as a balancing option in the Eurostat
pipeline, fixed and reliability-weighted cells, cross-entropy balancing
behind the same diagnostic interface, multi-sector generation from the
65-industry ESA core, and survey-driven household disaggregation. User
support, bug reports, and feature requests go through the repository's
GitHub Issues page; contributions are welcome and must pass the test
suite.
